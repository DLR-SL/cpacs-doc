CPACS viewer · search as a place

A tab of its own

The field leaves the strip above the tabs and becomes the content of a third tab, Search, which stands there whether anything is being searched or not. That settles what the results are — a place, not an overlay. Under the field stands one line of counted chips — All, Elements, Types, Attributes — so whoever prefers to click narrows in one press and reads what each kind would leave without asking. The syntax behind those chips moves into the existing ?, whose strip now has a second line. No new button anywhere. What is left open is where the rows themselves appear: three answers, drawn at the viewer's real scale.

Fixed · the strip

Three tabs, all permanent: Tree, Handbook, Search. None appears or vanishes mid-query, so the strip never changes width under the reader's hand.

Fixed · the field

The field sits inside the Search tab, the chips on the line below it. The ◐ and ? it used to share a row with are page chrome and move to the far end of the strip.

Fixed · the syntax

The prefixes stop being a title attribute and join the ? strip, on a second line under the tree's keys. The chips write the same forms into the field.

Fixed · the query survives

Opening a result goes to the Tree tab. The Search tab keeps its query and its rows, and its count says so — the list is a page, not a flash.

The syntax, and where it is taught

All of these are already in viewer.js. What they lack is a place to be read: today it is a line under the chips that only a reader who has already narrowed by hand ever sees, plus the field's title. Here they go into the ? strip — the one 0010 already put behind that button for the tree's keys — as a second line under them, with the forms set as text rather than as kbd keys so nobody looks for a key labelled type:. The chips under the field are the same switch with a mouse on it: pressing one writes its form into the field, so the field and the chips can never disagree. What this study does and does not settle

Settled by the premise, not by the variant: the results are a place with a name; the strip stops changing width; the syntax has a home; a query survives the click that opens a result. Those four hold in T1, T2 and T3 alike, and none of them depends on where the rows are drawn.

Not settled here: the truncation. The room a row has for a path is a function of the column it stands in, and naming that column does not widen it. T1 measures option A to the pixel — 352 px, 41 of 60 cut, 0.9 % — because it is option A with the field moved inside; T2 measures option B — 529 px, 5.6 %; T3 with its rail closed measures option D — 1,031 px, 71 %. The grouping question in ToDos.md — one row per name with its places listed, 2,223 names behind 53,691 entries — is still the decision that moves this by a factor of three, and it is still the one to take first.

The new cost the tab introduces is that the field is one click away where today it is always on screen. / answers that for whoever knows it and for nobody else — the count on the tab label is what has to carry it otherwise.

The chips are cheap and the syntax is free. Two earlier drafts of this study put the two together and paid for it: as a block of five explained rows under the field it took six of twenty rows in T1 and, as a rail beside the rows in T3, 326 px of path — which dropped whole paths from 71 % to 21 %, eating exactly what T3 is built for; as a panel hanging off its own filter button it cost no row, but then the counts were behind a press and the field row carried a second control. Split in two, each half lands where it belongs: the chips are one line — one row of eighteen in T1, nothing in T2 or T3 — and they show the counts unasked, which is what a reader wants while narrowing. The syntax costs nothing at all, because it moves into a strip that already exists and that a reader opens once, learns, and closes. Opening it costs four rows in T1 and none in T2 or T3.
